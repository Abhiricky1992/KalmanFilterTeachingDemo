Motivation: Why Do We Need a Kalman Filter?

Suppose an aircraft moves approximately with constant velocity. How can we estimate its state when both the motion model and the GPS measurements are uncertain?

Aircraft Motion, Prediction, and Measurement
The aircraft starts from a known initial state.

1. Predict Using a Motion Model

\[ \mathbf{x}_k= \begin{bmatrix} p_k\\[2pt] v_k \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{aligned} p_{k+1} &= p_k + v_k\Delta t\\ v_{k+1} &= v_k \end{aligned} \]

If the aircraft truly follows a constant-velocity model, then the model alone predicts the state accurately.

2. Measure Using GPS

\[ z_k = p_k \]

If the GPS measurement were perfect, then the measured position alone would accurately represent the state.

3. In Reality, Both Are Uncertain

Process uncertainty: wind, unmodeled acceleration, and simplifications in the motion model.

Measurement uncertainty: GPS noise, bias, and imperfect sensing.

We therefore need a framework that combines the model, the measurements, and their uncertainties to estimate the state correctly.
This is exactly what the Kalman Filter does.
Reference: Crassidis & Junkins, Optimal Estimation of Dynamic Systems.

Mathematical Modeling: Motion and Uncertainty

For simplicity, consider the simple aircraft motion at a constant altitude

1. What Do We Want to Estimate?

\[ \mathbf{x}_k = \begin{bmatrix} p_k\\ v_k \end{bmatrix} \]

\(p_k\): Aircraft position    \(v_k\): Aircraft velocity

3. Measurement Model

\[ z_k = H \mathbf{x}_k + \nu_k \]

In our simplified GPS measurement model position, H = [1 0]. The measurement contains an error \(\nu_k\).

We measure position, but want both position and velocity.

2. Process Model

\[ \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \underbrace{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \Delta t\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{F} \mathbf{x}_k + B\mathbf{u}_k + \Gamma\mathbf{w}_k \]

\(F\): State-transition matrix. \(B\): Input matrix. \(\Gamma\): Process-noise distribution matrix

\(\mathbf{u}_k\): Known control input. \(\mathbf{w}_k\): Process noise, representing unmodeled dynamics and disturbances.

For our constant-velocity example: \(\mathbf{u}_k=\mathbf{0}\).

4. Describing Uncertainty

\[ \mathbf{w}_k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},Q), \qquad \nu_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0,R) \]

Q: Covariance of process noise. Describes model-error uncertainty.

R: Variance of measurement noise. Describes GPS uncertainty.

  • Diagonal terms of Q: Variance of the state errors produced by the model
  • Off-diagonal terms of Q: Correlation between process noise errors
Given uncertain predictions and measurements, how should we combine them to estimate the state?
Reference: Crassidis & Junkins, Optimal Estimation of Dynamic Systems.

Kalman Filter: Prediction Step

Starting from the previous estimate, we use the motion model to predict the next state and propagate its uncertainty.

1. State Propagation

\[ \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k^- = F\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1}^+ +B\mathbf{u}_{k-1} \]

Propagate the previous corrected state estimate using the dynamics and the known control input. In our example, \(\mathbf{u}_{k-1}=\mathbf{0}\).

2. Defining the Covariance Matrix \(P\)

\[ P = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ (\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}) (\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}})^T \right] \]
  • Diagonal terms: variances of individual state errors
  • Off-diagonal terms: correlation/covariance between errors
  • Larger \(P\): less confidence in the estimate

3. Covariance Propagation

\[ P_k^- = FP_{k-1}^+F^T + \Gamma Q\Gamma^T \]

The uncertainty in the state estimate is also propagated. The process noise covariance \(Q\) adds uncertainty due to model imperfections and disturbances.

Conceptual View of Uncertainty Propagation
The ellipse is a conceptual picture of the covariance: after prediction, uncertainty typically grows because the process model is not perfect.
After prediction, we have a best forecast of the state and its uncertainty. Next, we will use a measurement to correct this prediction.
Reference: Crassidis & Junkins, Optimal Estimation of Dynamic Systems.

Kalman Filter: Correction Step

We now use the GPS measurement and its uncertainty to correct the predicted aircraft state.

1. Measurement Innovation

\[ \mathbf{r}_k = \mathbf{z}_k-H\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k^- \]

The innovation measures the difference between the GPS observation and the predicted measurement.

2. Kalman Gain: Minimum-Variance Estimation

The Kalman gain is chosen to minimize the estimation-error variance after measurement correction.

\[ \boxed{ K_k=P_k^-H^T(HP_k^-H^T+R)^{-1} } \]

The relative uncertainties determine how much the GPS measurement influences the prediction.

3. Correct the State Estimate

\[ \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k^+ =\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k^-+K_k\mathbf{r}_k \]

Update the estimated position and velocity using the measurement innovation.

4. Correct the Covariance

\[ P_k^+=(I-K_kH)P_k^- \]

Incorporating measurement information reduces uncertainty in the relevant state directions.

Key Takeaways: Kalman Filtering

What have we learned about estimating the state of a dynamic system?

1. Dynamic Models Predict Motion

A mathematical model predicts how the system state evolves, but model imperfections introduce uncertainty.

State prediction + covariance propagation

2. Measurements Provide Information

Sensors such as GPS provide observations of the system, but measurements are affected by noise.

Measurement model + measurement uncertainty

3. The Kalman Gain Balances Both

The Kalman gain determines how strongly measurements correct the prediction, based on their relative uncertainties.

Minimum-variance estimation

4. Estimation Is a Repeated Process

Prediction and correction are performed sequentially to continuously refine the state estimate and its uncertainty.

Predict → Correct → Repeat
The Kalman filter combines dynamic models and noisy measurements to obtain a more reliable estimate of the system state.
Reference: Crassidis & Junkins, Optimal Estimation of Dynamic Systems.
